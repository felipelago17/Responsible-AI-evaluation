# Principles Reference

**Source:** UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021), adopted by the General Conference at its 41st session, November 2021. All 193 UNESCO Member States voted in favour.

!!! quote
    "AI ethics should be approached from a human-centred perspective, placing the human being and the full realisation of their dignity, capabilities and well-being at the centre, while recognising the agency and rights of individuals and communities. AI should serve individuals, communities and societies, fostering a more just, sustainable, peaceful and inclusive world." — *UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021)*

---

## The 4 Core Values

The Recommendation identifies four overarching values that underpin all 10 principles. These values are not principles to be applied independently but the normative foundation from which the principles are derived.

### Value 1 — Respect, Protection and Promotion of Human Rights, Fundamental Freedoms, and Human Dignity

AI systems should uphold and advance internationally recognised human rights, including the rights to life, liberty, security, and dignity. AI must not be used as a tool to undermine democratic institutions, suppress dissent, or erode civil liberties. This value recognises that human dignity is inherent and inalienable, and that AI systems — however beneficial their intended purposes — must not instrumentalise human beings or treat them as data points rather than persons.

### Value 2 — Environment and Ecosystem Flourishing

AI development and deployment must be ecologically sustainable and must not compromise the health of natural systems on which all life depends. This value extends ethical consideration beyond the human sphere to the environment and other species. It recognises that the computational resources required by AI — energy, water, rare minerals, hardware — carry real ecological costs that must be accounted for and minimised.

### Value 3 — Ensuring Diversity and Inclusiveness

AI systems should reflect and serve the full diversity of humanity, not only the populations that are well-represented in training data or that have access to digital technologies. This value demands active effort to include marginalised groups in AI governance, to design systems that work equitably across languages, cultures, and abilities, and to prevent AI from concentrating power and opportunity among already-advantaged groups.

### Value 4 — Living in Peaceful, Just and Interconnected Societies

AI should contribute to social cohesion, the peaceful resolution of conflicts, and the strengthening of just institutions. It must not be used to polarise communities, spread disinformation, or enable authoritarian control. This value emphasises that AI is not merely a technical artefact but a social technology whose effects on social trust, democratic discourse, and global cooperation must be actively shaped.

## The 10 Principles

| # | Principle | One-line Definition | EIA Part(s) Addressed | Related Regulatory Instruments |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Proportionality and Do No Harm** | Use AI only when benefits outweigh risks; avoid unacceptable harms | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Arts. 9, 27; NIST AI RMF Map, Measure |
| 2 | **Safety and Security** | Ensure technical robustness, reliability, and resistance to adversarial attack | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Arts. 9, 15; ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A.6 |
| 3 | **Fairness and Non-Discrimination** | Treat all individuals equitably; do not perpetuate unjust discrimination | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Art. 10; OECD P1.4; NIST AI RMF Measure 2.5 |
| 4 | **Sustainability** | Minimise environmental footprint; support ecological flourishing | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Recital 48; ISO/IEC 42001 A.9 |
| 5 | **Right to Privacy and Data Protection** | Respect privacy rights; comply with data protection law throughout the data lifecycle | Part 1, Part 2, Part 3 | GDPR; EU AI Act Arts. 10, 27; ISO/IEC 42001 A.7 |
| 6 | **Human Oversight and Determination** | Retain meaningful human control over AI systems and high-stakes decisions | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Arts. 9, 14; CoE HUDERIA Stage 4 |
| 7 | **Transparency and Explainability** | Disclose AI use, capabilities, limitations, and decision logic | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Arts. 13, 50; NIST AI RMF Govern 1.1 |
| 8 | **Responsibility and Accountability** | Assign clear accountability; provide redress for harm | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Arts. 16–29; ISO/IEC 42001 Clause 5.3 |
| 9 | **Awareness and Literacy** | Ensure stakeholders understand AI well enough to engage critically | Part 2, Part 3 | EU AI Act Art. 4; OECD P1.5 |
| 10 | **Multi-stakeholder and Adaptive Governance and Collaboration** | Govern AI inclusively and adapt governance as technology and society evolve | Part 1, Part 2, Part 3 | NIST AI RMF Govern; CoE HUDERIA Stage 5; OECD P2 |

## Relationship Between Values and Principles

The 10 principles are not isolated requirements. Each is grounded in one or more of the 4 core values:

- **Value 1 (Human Rights and Dignity):** Principles 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8
- **Value 2 (Environment):** Principle 4
- **Value 3 (Diversity and Inclusiveness):** Principles 3, 9, 10
- **Value 4 (Peaceful, Just Societies):** Principles 2, 8, 10

Some principles span multiple values — Fairness (P3) serves both human dignity and inclusiveness; Accountability (P8) serves both human rights and just societies.
